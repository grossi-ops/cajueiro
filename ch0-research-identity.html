Chapter 0 · Before the First Paper

Creating Your Research Identity

One email. One name. Everywhere. — Hour House · Newark, NJ · Adult ESL

Before you write a single equation, before you submit a single paper, you need one thing: a consistent identity that follows you across every platform where research lives. This lesson shows you how to build it in one afternoon.
A2–B1 60 min Reading · Speaking · Writing Academic English · Digital Literacy Practical
"What you see is never the generator.
The fruit exposes the nut. The nut tends to a forest."
OVERVIEW Why Your Research Identity Matters

Your teacher, Pablo Nogueira Grossi, is an independent researcher. He lives in the Forest Hill neighborhood of Newark's North Ward. He works at a language school in Elizabeth, NJ. He has no university department, no laboratory, no institution paying for his research.

And yet his papers are published on Zenodo. His code is on GitHub. His ORCID connects everything. His research was presented in Natal, Brazil, at the Sociedade Brasileira de Matemática.

How? One email. One name. The same identity everywhere.

This chapter shows you how to do the same thing. You do not need to be at a university. You need to be consistent.

The Rule Create one Gmail address. Use it for everything in your research life: ORCID, GitHub, Zenodo, ResearchGate, grant applications. Do not use your work email, your school email, or a personal email that might change. The Gmail is yours forever.
STAGE 1 Vocabulary
10 minutes

Study these words. You will use them today and for the rest of your research life.

WordMeaningExample
identity who you are in a system; your name + email + number "My research identity is the same on every platform."
credential proof that you are who you say you are; a login, a certificate "My ORCID is my academic credential."
platform a website or service where you create an account "GitHub is a platform for sharing code."
repository a place where you store and share files, code, or papers "My papers are in my Zenodo repository."
consistent always the same; not changing "Use a consistent name across all platforms."
profile your public page on a platform; what others see about you "Fill in your GitHub profile so people know who you are."
DOI Digital Object Identifier — a permanent link to a paper or file "My paper's DOI is doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19984522"
ORCID Open Researcher and Contributor ID — your unique researcher number "My ORCID is 0009-0000-6496-2186"

STAGE 3 Build Your Identity — Step by Step
25 minutes · Hands-on

Follow these steps in order. Use the same name and the same Gmail for every account.

Before you begin Decide on your research name now. Use your full legal name — the same name you would put on a paper. Example: Pablo Nogueira Grossi, not Pablo G., not P. Grossi. Write it here: ___________________________
1
Create a Gmail address for research

Go to gmail.com. Create a new account using your full name if possible — for example pablongrossi@gmail.com. If your name is taken, add a number or your city: pablongrossi.newark@gmail.com. Write it down. This is your research email forever.

→ gmail.com
2
Create your ORCID

Go to orcid.org. Click Register. Use your Gmail. Use your full legal name. ORCID gives you a 16-digit number — write it down. This number is yours for life. It follows you if you change jobs, change countries, change your email.

→ orcid.org
3
Create a GitHub account

Go to github.com. Sign up with your Gmail. Choose a username that is close to your name — for example pngrossi or pgrossi-research. GitHub is where your code, writing, and projects will live. It is free. Your teacher's GitHub is at github.com/TOTOGT.

→ github.com
4
Create a Zenodo account

Go to zenodo.org. Click Log in with GitHub — use the GitHub account you just created. Zenodo is where you will publish papers and receive a DOI. It is free, run by CERN, and permanent. Your paper will be there in 50 years.

→ zenodo.org
5
Connect your ORCID to Zenodo

In Zenodo, go to your profile settings. Find the ORCID section. Click Connect. Log in to ORCID and authorize the connection. Now when you publish a paper on Zenodo, it automatically appears in your ORCID profile. One action, two places updated.

→ zenodo.org/account/settings/linkedaccounts
6
Write your research identity card

On a piece of paper, write: your full name, your research Gmail, your ORCID number, your GitHub username, your Zenodo profile URL. Keep this card. Put it in your notebook. This is who you are as a researcher.

→ no link needed — pen and paper
STAGE 4 Discussion Cards
10 minutes · Groups of 3–4
Card A — The Name

"Use a consistent name across all platforms."

What name will you use for research? Is it different from the name people call you every day? Why might it be important to use your full legal name? What problems could happen if you use different names in different places?

Card B — The Location

"Newark, NJ. No university. Still published."

Your teacher's papers say "Hour House ESL, Newark, NJ" as the affiliation. Not MIT. Not Harvard. What does this mean for you? Where are you right now? Can that be the starting point of your research identity?

Card C — The Number

"0009-0000-6496-2186"

Your ORCID number is yours for life. It does not change if you move, if you change your name, if you change your email. Why is a permanent number more reliable than a name or email? What else in your life has a permanent number?

Card D — The Seed

"The nut tends to a forest."

Today you are creating one Gmail, one ORCID, one GitHub. That is the seed. Your teacher started the same way. Where do you want your research identity to be in five years? What is the forest?

STAGE 5 Exit Writing
5 minutes · Individual

Write 3–5 sentences answering ONE of the following:

  1. Option A — Your Identity Card: Write your research name, your Gmail, your ORCID number, and your GitHub username. Then write one sentence about why you chose that Gmail address.
  2. Option B — The Comparison: How is a research identity similar to an identity document — like a passport or a driver's license? How is it different?
  3. Option C — The Forest: "The nut tends to a forest." What does this mean to you as a researcher starting today? What is the first root you are putting into the ground?
